Tip 03 · what you are paid

Ask Price

Seven conversations that set what you are paid, each carried through to the second move - the reply you get, and what you say to it. That is where these are decided and where most advice stops.

Standing Order argues that what you install beats what you know, and it is right about almost all of personal finance. These are the exceptions: a handful of moments where one sentence moves more than any default can, because there is no default that asks on your behalf.

/

Standing and limit

None of this is evidence. It is craft, and the literature on negotiation is thinner and far more contested than anything in Tare, so nothing above carries a number I would be willing to source. Treat it the way Standing Order treats its own weakest track: worth doing, not worth citing.

One thing here is arithmetic rather than a finding, and it is the reason the stakes are higher than a single conversation. A raise applies to the base that every later raise is taken against. Skip one and you have not lost that raise, you have moved the whole path down - the next percentage comes off a smaller number, so does the one after it, and so, usually, does the offer at your next job, because it anchors on what you are on now.

The asks are also not equally safe for everyone who might make them. Whether a flat number reads as confidence or as a problem depends on things that are not your delivery, and being told to just ask by someone for whom it was cheap is its own kind of unhelpful. What is portable is the preparation: the number decided before the room, the evidence written in their units, the window picked off a calendar. That part survives a room that is not on your side.
