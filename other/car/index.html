Port Injection

Five cars a year from 1995 to 2025, ranked for the person who does their own spanner work. Australian-delivered models, port injection first.

1995  →  2025

What you could actually buy

Every platform on this list, drawn across the years it was on sale here, and it follows the filters. On petrol the thesis is visible in the shape: dense for two decades, thinning sharply after 2016, and what carries port injectors into the 2020s is a handful of live-axle four wheel drives plus Toyota running port and direct together. On diesel the story is a clean handover, mechanical injection stopping dead around 2007 and common rail taking the whole field. A squared-off left edge means the model was already on sale before 1995.

What this ranks for

  1. Parts, decades out. An OEM supply or a real aftermarket, not one seller with a used one.
  2. Access. Can you reach the thing that fails without pulling the engine. A transverse V6 rear bank is a penalty.
  3. Documentation. A factory manual in circulation and a forum that quotes torque specs.
  4. Predictability. Known failure points beat mystery electronics. A car with a famous weak spot is easier to own than one with none listed.
  5. Tooling. No dealer-only scan tool to bleed brakes, register a battery or reset a service light.
  6. Structure. A body that survives the sun and holds a jack point where you need one.

A timing belt is not a demerit here. A belt is a scheduled front-of-engine job on a known interval for a few hundred dollars in parts. A chain has no interval, so it gets deferred until it rattles, and by then the job is often sump-off or engine-out. The chain advantage belongs to whoever pays a shop and sells the car at 150,000 km. If you are doing the work yourself the belt is the better deal, and the timing filter below is a preference rather than a ranking.

A famous weak spot is not a demerit either. A documented failure with cheap parts and a written procedure is easier to own than a car with nothing listed against it, because nothing listed usually means nobody has published the fix yet. That is why the EJ Subarus are here despite their head gaskets.

Why the years are not comparable

Port injection is not a discriminator for most of this range. From 1995 to about 2006 nearly every petrol car sold here was port injected, so the filter selects nothing and these years are ranked on mechanics alone.

Direct injection arrives around 2007 and spreads. By 2016 the constraint has inverted and port injection is the scarce thing. By 2024 a new port-injected petrol car in Australia is a Jimny, a Pentastar Jeep or a Toyota that keeps port injectors as half of a dual system.

Entries marked dual run port and direct injectors on the same engine, Toyota’s D-4S being the common case. They keep the intake valves washed and keep a low-pressure circuit alive, which is most of what you wanted from port injection, but they add a high-pressure pump and a second set of injectors. Check before you buy

Engine codes and injection types are the load-bearing claims on this page, and Australian-delivered specification sometimes differs from the same model sold in the United States or Japan. Read the engine code off the compliance plate or the block on the actual car, then confirm the fuel system against a factory manual for that market and build date.

Mid-generation changes are common and rarely advertised. Subaru moved the naturally aspirated boxers from the EJ to the FB inside a single model code, and Mazda swapped the MZR for Skyactiv inside the BL Mazda 3.

What is deliberately missing

Diesels. Every modern common-rail diesel is direct injected by definition, so the whole category falls outside the question even though a 1HZ or a 1KZ-TE is a fine thing to own and fix.

Cars whose parts supply has already gone. A platform can be mechanically simple and still be a bad long-term prospect once the only source for a wear item is a wrecker.

Rankings within a year are a judgement call, not a measurement. The engine codes and injection types are checkable facts. The order is an opinion about what your weekends will feel like.
